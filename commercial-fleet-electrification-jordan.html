FLEET ENGINEERING · DECISION FRAMEWORK

Commercial fleet electrification should begin with route and dwell data, not a generic vehicle-range target. A suitable vehicle must complete its duty with payload and reserve, return to a place where charging is available, and fit the site's electrical and operating constraints.

Screen routes and vehicles before procurement

Segment the fleet by vehicle class and mission: daily kilometres, stop frequency, payload, speed, grade, idle/accessory load, return time, and days of operation. Match these records against candidate vehicle specifications for the actual year, trim, battery, and market. Manufacturer cycle efficiency is a starting point, not a substitute for route data.

For each route, calculate energy per day and its uncertainty. Check the least-efficient expected day, battery usable window, ambient temperature, cabin or cargo cooling, and a reserve for detours and missed charging. A route that fits the nominal range may still fail operationally if there is no charging dwell or contingency vehicle.

Illustrative van-fleet energy check

Assumptions, not measured Jordan fleet data: 10 delivery vans; 140 km per vehicle per day; 0.22 kWh/km traction consumption; AC charging efficiency 90%. Per-van battery energy = 140 × 0.22 = 30.8 kWh/day. Site energy is 30.8/0.90 = 34.2 kWh/van-day; fleet energy is approximately 342 kWh/day.

If all 10 vans charge over an eight-hour window, the fleet's average charging power is 342/8 = 42.8 kW. This is an energy average, not the service peak: actual simultaneous charging, building load, charger control, and arrival times determine peak kW. A charger schedule or interval simulation is required before specifying a transformer.

Depot charging and grid integration

Build a time-series charging schedule from vehicle arrival, required departure SOC, charger efficiency, charge curve, and site load. Then test managed charging to reduce coincidence while preserving route readiness. DOE fleet guidance identifies duty cycles, vehicle models, peak demand, garaging, and off-site charging availability as core infrastructure planning factors.[1]

Compare options: AC overnight depot charging, higher-power opportunity charging, shared public charging, battery-buffered charging, and a mixed-fleet transition. Evaluate reliability, connector standards, charger maintenance, access control, cable handling, and whether a charger fault strands a critical route.

Jordan-specific tariff, licensing, and connection assumptions must be verified against current regulator/distributor documents. Store the document, effective date, customer class, and tariff in the analysis. Do not reuse a historic tariff as if it were current.

Total cost, emissions, and implementation

Compare alternatives on a consistent service basis, such as cost per vehicle-kilometre or delivered parcel: vehicle and battery capital, financing, energy, charger/depot work, utility upgrades, demand charges, maintenance, tires, insurance, taxes, downtime, and residual value. Apply matched annual kilometres and service availability to the combustion baseline.

For emissions, state whether the boundary is tailpipe, electricity generation, or full life cycle. Use a dated Jordan grid factor and disclose whether it is an average or marginal factor; do not call an EV “zero-emission” without specifying the boundary.

  1. Instrument representative routes before procurement.
  2. Run a controlled pilot across seasons and payload conditions.
  3. Track energy per route, charging kWh, vehicle availability, maintenance, and service completion.
  4. Expand only after validating charger reliability, grid capacity, technician training, and battery safety procedures.
