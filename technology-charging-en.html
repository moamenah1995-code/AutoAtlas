Charging Infrastructure

Charging is more than the visible unit: it is an energy source, safe connection, grid interface, load-management system, dependable service, and clear user experience.

01 — ALTERNATING CURRENT

AC charging and destinations

With AC charging, the vehicle's onboard charger converts incoming electricity for the battery, so actual power is constrained by both onboard equipment and the supply. It commonly serves longer dwell times at homes, workplaces, and some public parking sites.

02 — DIRECT CURRENT

DC and high-power charging

A DC charger converts power outside the vehicle and supplies it to the car. Charging power can change during a session with battery temperature, state of charge, and vehicle and charger limits; the rated peak is not necessarily the session average.

03 — INTEROPERABILITY

Connectors and communication

Connectors and standards vary by market and vehicle. Check the match between the vehicle inlet and charger, communications protocol, and local certification requirements. Good station information states connector, power, and availability before a driver arrives.

04 — GRID AND OPERATIONS

Load planning and reliability

Coincident demand, service capacity, transformers, and utility requirements shape site cost. Load management and scheduling can share available capacity; operations need fault monitoring, payments, support, maintenance, and accessible spaces.

From planning to service

Before construction, assess traffic, dwell time, expected demand, land, permits, and grid connection. Compare connection and capacity costs with likely use, and plan maintenance and accessibility. Installed port count alone does not measure charging-network quality.
