ENGINEERING REFERENCE

Charging power is bounded by the site supply, EVSE, cable/inlet, vehicle onboard charger (AC), battery management system, and battery acceptance curve. The delivered power is the lowest applicable limit at each instant. Values below are worked examples, not universal product ratings; use the vehicle and equipment nameplates and local electrical code for design.

Single-phase, three-phase, and power factor

For single-phase sinusoidal AC, real power is approximately P = V × I × PF. For balanced three-phase supply, P = √3 × VLL × IL × PF, where VLL is line-to-line voltage. Power factor (PF = real power/apparent power) captures phase displacement and, in practical nonlinear converters, distortion effects. A high PF reduces current and kVA for a given kW but does not remove cable, protection, voltage-drop, or thermal limits. The EVSE signals a permissible current; it is not necessarily the AC/DC conversion device.

Charging efficiency depends on the measurement boundary, load, temperature, power electronics, battery conditioning, and auxiliary loads. In the worked example below, 90% AC and 95% DC wall-to-pack efficiencies are explicit assumptions, not guaranteed fleet averages. NREL cost modeling uses a 15% loss assumption for transformer and charging equipment in one heavy-duty charging analysis; actual projects need measured or equipment-specific values (NREL assumptions).

CHARGING CURVE

Power versus state of charge

The vehicle requests and accepts power within battery, charger, and site constraints. A typical DC session rises to a limited plateau and tapers as SOC increases; temperature, cell design, preconditioning, voltage, and charger sharing change the curve.

Worked example: 60 kWh pack, 20% to 80%

Energy stored = 60 kWh × (0.80 − 0.20) = 36 kWh. For 11 kW three-phase AC with an 11 kW onboard charger and assumed 90% wall-to-pack efficiency: pack power ≈ 9.9 kW; idealized time = 36 / 9.9 = 3.64 h (about 3 h 38 min). Grid energy ≈ 36 / 0.90 = 40 kWh.

For DC, assume an 85 kW average at the battery over this SOC window (not the 150 kW station nameplate): 36 / 85 h = 0.424 h ≈ 25 min. At an assumed 95% wall-to-pack efficiency, grid energy ≈ 36 / 0.95 = 37.9 kWh. Real time can be longer because of taper, cold/hot battery conditioning, auxiliary loads, pauses, power sharing, and SOC display rounding. This estimate uses average pack power, so do not apply efficiency a second time to the time calculation.

SITE AND GRID ENGINEERING

Distribution capacity and site load calculations

From charger nameplate to service capacity

Start with existing measured peak load, charger count and ratings, duty schedules, diversity/coincidence, building loads, power factor, conversion losses, and any storage or generation. Managed charging can enforce a site import ceiling, but the utility must review transformer, feeder, voltage regulation, protection, fault current, harmonics, and interconnection limits.

Example load screen: 10 ports × 11 kW = 110 kW connected load. If a controller guarantees no more than 6 ports charge at full power simultaneously, EV load is capped at 66 kW; add the building’s coincident peak (for example 80 kW) to estimate 146 kW real-power demand before design margins and kVA/PF checks. This is a planning screen, not an equipment-sizing calculation: use code-required demand rules, interval data, utility studies, conductor/protection calculations, and qualified engineering review.

Demand charges and grid impact

Some commercial tariffs bill a monthly peak kW or kVA in addition to energy (kWh), sometimes with time windows or ratchets. A rarely used fast charger can therefore incur a large cost relative to its delivered energy. NREL analysis identifies demand charges as a major cost driver for low-utilization DC fast-charging sites; the effect is tariff- and site-specific (NREL REopt charging-site analysis). At distribution level, clustered unmanaged charging may raise local peaks, transformer loading, voltage drop, and upgrade needs; impacts depend on feeder conditions, timing, and adoption, not EV count alone. NREL notes EV charging’s local grid effects require distribution-specific analysis (NREL managed-charging review).

Managed and bidirectional charging

Managed charging shifts or modulates charging against departure needs, charger limits, site import caps, tariffs, and grid signals. It can reduce coincident peak and defer some upgrades if customers, control systems, and utility operations support it; it cannot guarantee a grid benefit at every location. V2G exports vehicle energy through compatible bidirectional vehicle and EVSE hardware, communications, metering, protection, interconnection approval, and market compensation. Account for conversion losses, battery degradation, reserve SOC, user mobility, export limits, and fail-safe behavior. DOE describes these compatibility requirements and service limits in its managed and bidirectional charging guide.

OPERATIONAL CONSTRAINTS

Engineering limitations and deployment practice

  • Vehicle acceptance: onboard charger rating caps AC; for DC, battery voltage/current, SOC, temperature, cell limits, and BMS requests govern instantaneous acceptance.
  • Thermal and electrical losses: cables/connectors heat; power conversion and battery conditioning consume energy. Nameplate kW is not battery kW.
  • Site constraints: transformer spare capacity, utility service lead time, easements, trenching, switchgear, protection, accessibility, parking turnover, and permitting can dominate cost and schedule.
  • Shared power: cabinets may split output among ports; the posted per-port maximum may not be simultaneous.
  • Availability: payment, network, connector, and maintenance faults reduce delivered service. Specify uptime measurement, repair response, accessibility, and queueing metrics.
  • Grid limits: local feeder and transformer impacts require utility-specific studies; storage and managed charging can shift peaks but add capital, losses, controls, and operational dependencies.
DESIGN CHECKLIST

What a credible charging plan specifies

  1. Vehicle mix, battery sizes, arrival SOC, departure deadlines, daily energy, and seasonal conditions.
  2. Per-port and shared charger power, voltage/current envelope, efficiency boundary, connector/protocol, and metering.
  3. Interval site load, service/transformer/feeder limits, demand tariff, protection, power quality, utility upgrades, and expansion path.
  4. Control strategy and fail-safe behavior for communications loss, controller outage, and emergency load shed.
  5. V2G capability only where vehicle, EVSE, interconnection, tariff/market, and customer consent are confirmed.
  6. Commissioning tests and ongoing KPIs: successful sessions, availability, energy, utilization, queuing, peak kW, and repair time.
TECHNICAL QUESTIONS

Frequently asked questions

Why can a 150 kW station charge a vehicle at much less than 150 kW?

The station rating is only one limit. The vehicle may accept less because of battery SOC or temperature, pack voltage/current, onboard controls, or power shared with another port.

Can three-phase charging be three times faster?

Not automatically. Available phase current, onboard charger design, vehicle acceptance, site limits, and efficiency determine power. A vehicle with a single-phase onboard charger cannot use a three-phase supply as three-phase charging.

Does V2G mean every EV can power the grid?

No. Vehicle and EVSE must both support bidirectional export, and the installation needs compatible communications, metering, protection, utility approval, and a viable operating agreement.
