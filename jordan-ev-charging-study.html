INFRASTRUCTURE RESEARCH · DATED DATA

Charging access is a system property: vehicle compatibility, station location, connector count, power, operating status, utilization, reliability, pricing, and route spacing all matter. This page defines a consistent inventory so future counts can be compared without mixing stations, charging units, and individual connectors.

What an infrastructure baseline should report

Jordan's Energy and Minerals Regulatory Commission (EMRC) reported in January 2025 that 142 licensed and operating stations were available nationally, including 117 public and 25 private stations; it also noted more than 620 stations with construction permits still completing licensing steps. Those are distinct regulatory statuses and a dated snapshot, not a current live-map count.[1]

Recommended data collection method

  1. Collect the regulator's licensing/public station data and the applicable EV charging rules; capture publication date and status fields.
  2. Normalize site coordinates, operator, public/private access, hours, payment method, connector type, rated output, connector count, and shared-power behavior.
  3. Verify a sample by telephone or field visit. Record observation date and whether the station was accessible, functioning, occupied, and able to initiate a session.
  4. Map practical coverage by route and settlement, not just national totals. Show road-network distance to an operational charger, route detours, and redundancy where data allows.
  5. Keep planned, permitted, licensed, and verified operational sites in separate layers. Add a visible “last checked” date to every record.

A map pin alone cannot establish uptime, power delivery, connector compatibility, or successful payment. If those fields are not available, mark them “unknown” rather than assuming service.

Capacity and coverage are different metrics

For capacity planning, report installed kW per site and per connector, but distinguish nameplate power from simultaneous delivered power. Shared power cabinets, transformer limits, thermal derating, and vehicles' acceptance curves can constrain session output. For travel coverage, assess spacing against a vehicle's usable range under the route conditions, including reserve and outage contingencies.

EMRC publishes electricity-sector legislation and tariff instructions, including 2025 materials. Charging prices and distributor commissions can change; record the tariff schedule, customer class, and effective date alongside any cost estimate rather than copying a historic tariff into a “current” figure.[2]

A useful accessibility dashboard would pair public connector density with population/vehicle exposure, road-corridor coverage, operating hours, payment access, and uptime. A national average can hide gaps between Amman, intercity routes, rural towns, and tourist destinations.

Limits and next study steps

  • Published station totals may use different definitions and reporting dates.
  • A permit is not evidence of construction completion or operation.
  • Charger output varies with cabinet sharing, vehicle voltage/current limits, temperature, and battery state of charge.
  • Open map services may omit private stations and can contain stale entries.
  • Utilization and uptime require time-stamped session or inspection data, not site counts alone.

The next release should publish a downloadable, deduplicated site register with field definitions, source lineage, observation dates, and a correction process. Do not infer utilization or reliability until session data are available.

Design a charging site around energy, power and utilization

A charger count is not a capacity plan. This example shows how the same daily energy can produce very different grid peaks depending on charger ratings and control.

Did you know?

A 150 kW label is a maximum rating, not a promise that every vehicle will continuously receive 150 kW.

Worked calculation

At an ideal constant 600 kW, 540 kWh requires 0.9 h of aggregate full-power time. Real charging takes longer because sessions overlap imperfectly, vehicle acceptance tapers with state of charge and temperature, and conversion/auxiliary losses add energy. The 600 kW cap is a scenario target—not a transformer or feeder recommendation.

Interpretation and limits

A yearly energy total does not determine peak power, simultaneous demand, network voltage, or reliability. These calculations are reproducible planning examples based on the supplied manuscript. They are not measurements of a Jordanian operator, utility, or site. Confirm local tariffs, permits, equipment ratings, and connection conditions before design.
