FLEET SYSTEMS · FEASIBILITY FRAMEWORK

Bus electrification is a service and infrastructure design problem, not only a vehicle purchase. Range, passenger service, route scheduling, depot dwell time, charger power, grid connection, backup fleet, and procurement all need to work together.

Jordan context: distinguish targets from operating results

Jordan's Third National Energy Efficiency Action Plan lists a feasibility study for solar-powered electric-bus fleet pilots in Karak, Ma’an, and Tafila; a listed feasibility study is not evidence that a pilot fleet is operating. The same plan set a target for 32 of 187 Amman–Zarqa and Amman BRT buses to be electric by 2026 and described 15 electric buses within a 151-bus Amman City Bus second-phase initiative. The target date has passed; confirm procurement, commissioning, and actual in-service counts before describing the targets as achieved.[1] Ministry reporting in April 2024 described rollout preparations for Amman–Zarqa BRT, which documents transport infrastructure and operational preparations, not by itself electric-bus deployment.[2]

For Amman, start by identifying the operator, route, vehicle class, passenger load, schedule, and depot. Do not assume all public-transport services share the same duty cycle or procurement structure.

Route-energy and service analysis

  1. Obtain GPS traces, stop dwell, scheduled headways, elevation, passenger/load profile, and seasonal air-conditioning demand.
  2. Use bus-specific measured or manufacturer energy consumption; model worst-case route conditions rather than using a headline range.
  3. Check battery usable window, reserve, degradation allowance, HVAC load, and opportunity/depot charging options.
  4. Simulate vehicle blocks and charging windows while preserving service frequency, recovery time, and spare ratio.
  5. Compare depot charging, opportunity charging, and mixed fleets against utility service capacity, transformer upgrades, and resilience requirements.

Illustrative depot-energy calculation

Assumptions only: one bus travels 200 km in a service day and averages 1.2 kWh/km from its traction battery on the modeled duty cycle. Traction energy = 200 × 1.2 = 240 kWh/bus-day. At 90% assumed charger-to-battery efficiency, site energy is 240/0.90 = 267 kWh/bus-day. For 10 buses, this is 2.67 MWh/day before depot auxiliaries, charging-window constraints, reserve, and other loads.

This daily energy does not specify the required charger rating. If all vehicles return together, instantaneous demand can be much higher than average power; managed charging and a scheduled fleet model are needed to estimate the peak.

Economics, emissions, and service risks

Compare life-cycle cost per service-kilometre using vehicle and battery capital, charging equipment, grid upgrades, electricity tariff, demand charges where applicable, maintenance, residual value, and spare-fleet costs. Compare emissions using the electricity mix and a matched diesel-bus duty cycle; include vehicle and battery manufacture if the claim is lifecycle-based.

  • Route energy may rise with passenger load, hills, congestion, hot-weather air conditioning, and service interruptions.
  • Depot charging failures can affect an entire vehicle block; assess redundant connectors and dispatch contingencies.
  • High-power opportunity charging may reduce onboard battery size but increases site power and schedule sensitivity.
  • Battery replacement, warranty, fire strategy, emergency response, staff training, and end-of-life management must be included in procurement.

A credible pilot should report route, passenger/service, energy, charging, availability, and cost data over a representative operating period, with a comparable baseline and stated boundary.

Depot charging for a hypothetical Amman fleet

This transparent fleet scenario estimates daily traction energy first, then converts it into charger-side energy and average depot power. It is not an Amman Transit Authority fleet inventory or rollout plan.

Did you know?

The energy needed by a fleet and the connection power needed by its depot are different design quantities.

Worked calculation

Battery-side arithmetic: 37.5 + 26.0 = 63.5 MWh/day. With 90% wall-to-battery efficiency, site energy is 63.5/0.90 = 70.6 MWh/day. Delivering it in six hours requires 11.8 MW average. This does not size substations: measured arrival/departure times, route energy, charger curves, existing depot load, power factor, redundancy, and utility fault/voltage studies are required.

Interpretation and limits

A yearly energy total does not determine peak power, simultaneous demand, network voltage, or reliability. These calculations are reproducible planning examples based on the supplied manuscript. They are not measurements of a Jordanian operator, utility, or site. Confirm local tariffs, permits, equipment ratings, and connection conditions before design.
